A living group is a domain with a sink

The rules of Go are entirely local. Everything that matters in Go is not. I spent a while trying to work out how one becomes the other, and ended up somewhere unexpected: a graph Laplacian, a forest of little paths, and the observation that capturing a group is a spectral event.

Notes · September 2026

I play Go. Not well enough to be interesting about it, but well enough that I have spent a lot of evenings staring at a board wondering why the game feels the way it does.

Here is the thing that bothers me, stated as plainly as I can. The rules of Go fit on an index card, and every one of them is local. A point is black, white, or empty. Stones of the same colour that touch are one unit. If a unit has no empty point next to it, take it off the board. That is essentially all of it.

And yet nothing anyone says about Go is local. Players talk about territory, influence, thickness, a group being alive or dead, a wall facing the wrong way. These are statements about large regions of the board. None of them appear anywhere in the rules. They are not definitions; they are things that happen when you let the local rules run.

That is a familiar shape if you do condensed matter for a living. It is the shape of every emergence story we tell: magnets don't have magnetism written into the electron, water doesn't have freezing written into the molecule. You write down something local and boring and something global and interesting falls out. So the question I actually wanted to answer was not "can I build a Go AI" — people have done that considerably better than I would — but:

Can local rules make a system decide what counts as inside?

The rule that looks global but isn't

Start with the obvious encoding. Every point on the board is one of three things, so give it a variable that takes three values: −1 for white, 0 for empty, +1 for black. Physicists have studied that object to death — it's a spin-1 lattice gas — and on its own it gets you nothing surprising. Stones like to sit next to other stones; that's it.

The interesting rule is the survival rule. A group of stones must touch at least one empty point, or it dies. Read that carefully and it should make you uncomfortable, because it is a statement about a connected component — a global object. To check it you have to trace the whole group. There is no way to look at one stone and know.

Except there is, and this is the part I find genuinely lovely.

Take one colour's stones and build the obvious graph: a node per stone, an edge between touching stones. Every graph has a Laplacian, L, which is the discrete version of the operator that runs diffusion. Now add a diagonal matrix R that gives a stone a positive weight if it touches an empty point, and zero if it doesn't. Then:

det(L + R) > 0  ⇔  every group of that colour has a liberty

The proof is three lines. That determinant is zero exactly when the matrix has a zero mode, a vector it annihilates. The quadratic form is a sum of squares: differences along edges, plus the root weights. To kill it you need something constant across a whole connected group and zero wherever a stone touches empty space. That is possible only if some entire group touches no empty space at all — which is precisely a dead group.

So the rule that looked irreducibly global is a determinant. And determinants of this exact shape are not arbitrary: by a classical theorem, det(L + R) counts something. It counts the ways of drawing a forest of little paths on the stones such that every stone follows a path out to a liberty. Each dying group is a group where no such escape route can be drawn.

Capture is a spectral event

Once you have written it this way, a reinterpretation falls out that I did not expect and have not stopped thinking about.

L + R is what a mathematician would call a killed Laplacian: it describes a random walker wandering around on the stones of a group, who can escape whenever it reaches a stone that touches empty space. The liberties are absorbing boundaries. Drains.

A living group is a domain with a sink.

That is not a metaphor — it is the same equation. And it makes life and death, which in Go is a famously binary and famously difficult judgement, into something continuous. The smallest eigenvalue of that matrix, which I'll call the liberty gap, measures how freely the walker can escape. A fat healthy group with liberties on all sides has a large gap. A long snaking group whose only escape is through a narrow corridor has a small one, even if you can't see the problem yet. And at the exact moment of capture, the gap hits zero and the determinant vanishes.

Every Go player already knows this feeling: a group that is technically alive but that you do not trust. The gap is what that feeling would look like if you wrote it down.

Territory is a hole

The other half is territory, and here the right language turns out to be topology.

Don't look at the stones. Look at the empty space, and ask how many separate pieces it falls into. At the start of the game the answer is one — the whole board is one connected emptiness. As stones go down, walls form, and the emptiness starts breaking into chambers. Territory is just a chamber that only one colour touches.

There is a theorem — Alexander duality — that says the number of extra pieces you cut a surface into is exactly the number of independent loops in the thing doing the cutting. So counting territories and counting closed walls are literally the same computation, done from opposite sides. The board's own edge counts as one of those walls, which is the formal reason corner territory is cheap and centre territory is expensive: in the corner, two sides of your enclosure are already built.

I don't think this is a coincidence about Go so much as a fact about enclosure that Go happens to make vivid.

The actual question

Which brings me to what I actually want to know, and don't.

You can always cheat. You can write a model that gets rewarded for surrounding territory, run it, and watch it surround territory. That proves nothing; you put the answer in by hand.

The honest experiment is to write down the local energy — stones like neighbours, stones cost something to place — plus the survival constraint, and nothing that mentions area or enclosure at all. Then run it and ask whether large enclosed regions show up anyway.

Can a locally interacting system undergo a phase transition in the topology of its complement?

Phase transitions in density, in magnetisation, in order — those we understand. A phase transition in whether the empty space is connected is a stranger thing to ask for. If it happens, the interesting question is whether it needs an adversary: whether two players trying to beat each other produce enclosure that neither thermal equilibrium nor undirected random play ever would. My suspicion is yes, and suspicion is all it currently is.

So this is not a finished result. It is a well-posed question with the boring parts already proved, which is the stage of a project I enjoy most.

You can play against it

Because the model scores positions, it can pick moves — so I let it. The bot below has no tree search, no neural network, and no opening book. It scores every legal move by the energy of the resulting position, including the liberty-entropy term, and samples from the Boltzmann weight.

It is not strong. Any club player will dismantle it. But it is transparent in a way that a neural network is not: every curve plotted next to the board is a term the bot is actually playing by, and the sliders are the coefficients in the Hamiltonian. Turn up the clustering term and it huddles. Turn up the liberty term and it stops caring about territory and starts fighting. Drop the temperature to zero and it becomes rigid and deterministic and, oddly, worse.

Watching the liberty gap of your own group slide toward zero while you are busy elsewhere is, I think, the most useful thing in it.
